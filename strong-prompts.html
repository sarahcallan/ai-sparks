Anatomy of a strong prompt

In 15 minutes, turn a vague request into one that gets you a useful first draft.

Start here

A prompt is just instructions. The clearer the instructions, the less time you spend fixing the result.

You’ll need an AI tool open, like Copilot or Claude, and one task in mind. If you did “Where Does AI Fit?”, use the step you starred.

Same request, two ways

Vague

“Write an email about the deadline.”

Strong

You’re a friendly, organized program coordinator.

About a third of our study abroad students haven’t submitted their visa documents, due Friday. Last term’s reminder is pasted below so you can match its tone.

Write a reminder email, under 150 words, for students who haven’t submitted.

Subject line plus three short paragraphs. Warm but direct.

Cover the deadline, where to submit, and who to contact.

Don’t promise extensions. Don’t include student names.

What to notice

The vague version isn’t wrong. The AI just has to guess everything: tone, length, audience, and what matters. The strong version answers those questions up front.

It’s longer, but it takes about a minute to write. That minute saves you several rounds of “no, not like that.”

Six parts

🎭RoleWho should the AI act as? Start with “You’re a…”
🧭ContextSet the scene in one sentence. Paste or attach what it should use.
🎯TaskThe deliverable in one line: what, how long, and who it’s for.
📐FormatHeadings? Bullets? Word count? Tone?
✅Must coverThe topics it can’t leave out.
🚧ConstraintsWhat to leave out, what to check, what not to do.

You don’t need all six every time

A quick question might only need a Task. The more the output matters, the more parts you add.

Heard of Goal, Context, Source, Expectations?

That’s the framework used in Microsoft sessions. These six parts cover the same ground in more detail. Task is your Goal. Source lives inside Context. Format, Must cover, and Constraints are your Expectations.

🎯 TaskWrite a reminder email, under 150 words, for students who haven’t submitted.

Start with three

Role, Context, and Task do most of the work. Get these right first.

Tips

  • Role: pick someone who’d be good at this task, not just a job title.
  • Context: say what just happened and why it matters. If you have an example, a file, or data, paste or attach it here.
  • Task: name the thing, its length, and its audience in one line.

Don’t run it yet. You’ll compare versions in step 3.

Tips

  • Format: if you can picture the result, describe it. Length and tone matter most.
  • Must cover: list the facts or topics the output can’t miss. Short is fine.
  • Constraints: this is where you protect yourself. What should it avoid? What should it tell you to verify?

“Flag anything I should double-check” is worth adding to almost any prompt.

How to compare

Open two separate chats so the first answer doesn’t influence the second. Paste the vague prompt in one and your strong prompt in the other.

Still not right?

Don’t start over. Tell it what to change, like “Shorter, and lead with the deadline.” Then add that to your prompt for next time.

Your reusable prompt

You’re a [who would be great at this].

[What’s happening and why it matters.] [Paste or attach what to use.]

Write [what], [how long], for [who].

Format: [structure and tone].

Cover: [must-have topics].

Don’t [what to avoid]. Flag anything I should double-check.

Keep this

Copy the template into a note or document. Fill in the brackets each time you start a new task.

A tip for later

If you use the same prompt three times, save it. In tools like Copilot Notebooks or Claude Projects, you can store it with your examples so you never retype it. That’s “Teach it” from Where Does AI Fit?
